What IoT Security Protects: Safety, Availability & the Extended CIA Model

Explain why safety and availability often outrank confidentiality in CPS.

35 min read Level: MS foundation → advanced Status: Full lesson

1. Introduction

Ask a web security team what they're protecting and you'll hear "data." Ask an industrial control engineer the same question and you'll hear "people, and the process." Both are right — and IoT security sits exactly at the point where those two answers have to coexist.

2. Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Explain why safety and availability often outrank confidentiality in CPS.
  • Map a device's failure modes to real-world physical consequences.
  • Justify control choices using an extended CIA+Safety framework.

3. Prerequisites

Week 0 foundations, plus Weeks 0 of this course.

4. Core Concepts

The classic CIA triad — Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability — still applies, but IoT/CPS work requires an explicit fourth property: Safety. A leaked temperature reading (confidentiality loss) is rarely dangerous. A stuck-open valve or a car that won't brake (safety loss) can be. The ordering of priorities frequently inverts compared to traditional IT:

PropertyTypical IT priorityTypical CPS priority
ConfidentialityOften highestOften lowest
IntegrityHighHigh
AvailabilityImportantOften critical
SafetyNot modeledCan dominate every other property

This isn't a claim that confidentiality never matters in IoT — health-monitoring wearables are a clear counterexample — but the default weighting shifts, and a security program that copies IT priorities unmodified will under-invest in the properties that actually prevent harm.

7. Mathematical / Formal Foundations

A simple way to reason about priority: assign each property a rough "cost of failure" for a given device. For a smart thermostat, a one-hour confidentiality breach might cost little; a one-hour safety failure (stuck heating element) could be severe. The exercise isn't about precise numbers — it's about forcing an explicit conversation before defaults are chosen.

8. Practical Examples

Worked example: a connected insulin pump. Confidentiality failure (someone learns your glucose trend) is a real privacy harm but rarely life-threatening. Integrity or availability failure (wrong dose delivered, or pump stops responding during a critical window) can be fatal. Any control that trades availability for confidentiality here — like an aggressive lockout after failed logins — needs very careful thought.

9. Code / Worked Example

QUICK PRIORITY-WEIGHTING EXERCISE
For your device, rate 1-5 impact of a one-hour failure in each property:

Confidentiality: ___   (e.g., data exposure, privacy harm)
Integrity:       ___   (e.g., false readings, tampered commands)
Availability:    ___   (e.g., device stops responding)
Safety:          ___   (e.g., physical harm to people or environment)

Whichever property scores highest should visibly shape your top 3
security investments -- if it doesn't, revisit the investment list.

10. Security Perspective

Security engineers moving from IT to IoT/OT commonly over-apply confidentiality-first instincts — for example, encrypting everything at the cost of latency that a safety-critical control loop cannot tolerate. Recognizing the shift in priority is often the single biggest mindset adjustment this course asks of you.

11. Industry Perspective

Industrial safety standards (like IEC 61508) predate modern cybersecurity standards by decades and already formalize safety analysis — Week 15's OT/ICS material builds directly on that lineage, and increasingly, security and safety teams are merging their risk processes rather than running them in parallel.

12. Research Perspective

A growing body of research proposes "safety-security co-engineering" frameworks that analyze both properties in the same model rather than as separate workstreams — a response to the documented failure of siloed approaches to catch cross-cutting risks.

13. Case Study

A widely studied pattern in connected-vehicle research: aggressive confidentiality controls (strict certificate pinning with no graceful fallback) have, in some designs, caused availability failures in safety-relevant communication when a certificate issue occurred in the field — illustrating the real cost of applying IT-style priorities without adjustment.

14. Common Misconceptions

  • "Safety is just a subset of availability." — A device can be perfectly available and still unsafe (e.g., available but issuing wrong commands).
  • "CPS security means confidentiality doesn't matter." — It still matters for many devices (health data, location); it's the default weighting that shifts, not a blanket exemption.
  • "Adding safety analysis means starting from scratch." — Most organizations can extend existing threat-modeling processes rather than replacing them.

16. Glossary

CIA triad
Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability — the classical model of information security goals.
Safety (as a security property)
Freedom from conditions that could cause physical harm to people, property, or the environment.
Safety-security co-engineering
An approach that analyzes safety and security risks together rather than in separate silos.

17. Self-Assessment

1. Why is Safety added as a distinct property in the extended CIA model for CPS?

Safety captures physical-world harm potential that CIA alone doesn't model — a device can satisfy CIA and still be unsafe.

2. For a typical industrial actuator (e.g., a valve controller), which property most often needs the highest default weighting?

For actuating devices, wrong or unresponsive commands (integrity/safety failures) typically carry the highest real-world cost.
