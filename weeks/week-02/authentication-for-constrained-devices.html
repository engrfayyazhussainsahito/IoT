Authentication for Constrained Devices

Select an authentication scheme appropriate to a device's compute budget.

35 min read Level: MS foundation → advanced Status: Full lesson

1. Introduction

Choosing how a device proves who it is involves a genuine engineering trade-off, not just a security checkbox: every authentication scheme costs compute cycles, memory, and often power, and a scheme that's free on a server can be prohibitively expensive on a battery-powered sensor.

2. Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Select an authentication scheme appropriate to a device's compute budget.
  • Explain mutual TLS and pre-shared-key trade-offs for IoT.

3. Prerequisites

Week 0 foundations, plus Weeks 0–1 of this course.

4. Core Concepts

Three common patterns, from lightest to heaviest:

  • Pre-shared keys (PSK) — simplest and cheapest, but rotating a compromised key across a fleet with no per-device individuality is operationally hard, and a single extracted key can compromise every device sharing it.
  • Mutual TLS (mTLS) with per-device certificates — strong, individually revocable identity, but the handshake's asymmetric-crypto cost is real on constrained CPUs and its message size can matter on bandwidth-limited links.
  • OAuth2-style device flows — common for consumer products where a device needs to be authorized into a user's account; typically layered on top of, not a replacement for, device-level authentication.

Session resumption (reusing an abbreviated handshake for subsequent connections) is the standard way to keep mTLS's cost from being paid on every single connection, trading some forward-secrecy nuance for a large power savings.

7. Mathematical / Formal Foundations

A full TLS 1.3 handshake with ECDSA can cost tens of milliseconds of CPU time on a low-end microcontroller — potentially more energy than transmitting the actual sensor payload it's meant to protect. Session resumption reduces this to a few round trips with symmetric-only operations for subsequent connections.

8. Practical Examples

Worked example: a battery-powered leak sensor wakes once daily. A full mTLS handshake every wake would meaningfully shorten battery life. Session resumption (or, in extreme cases, a lightweight PSK scheme with per-device keys rather than a shared fleet key) may be the right trade-off, provided key rotation is still possible without a full handshake.

10. Security Perspective

The operational cost of key rotation is often the deciding factor in practice: a scheme that's cryptographically ideal but impossible to rotate at fleet scale without a truck roll or full reflash is, in practice, a scheme that never gets rotated.

11. Industry Perspective

Cloud IoT platforms increasingly offer managed device-identity services that handle certificate issuance, rotation, and revocation as a service — reducing the operational burden that has historically pushed teams toward weaker shared-key schemes out of convenience.

12. Research Perspective

Lightweight authentication protocol research (some feeding into the NIST Lightweight Cryptography effort covered in Week 3) specifically targets reducing handshake cost for constrained devices without falling back to shared-secret designs.

13. Case Study

Multiple consumer IoT product lines have shipped with a single hardcoded PSK shared across every unit sold — chosen for engineering simplicity — resulting in fleet-wide compromise once the key was extracted from any single retail unit.

14. Common Misconceptions

  • "Pre-shared keys are always insecure." — Per-device PSKs with proper rotation can be reasonable; the failure mode is sharing one key across an entire fleet.
  • "mTLS is always too expensive for embedded devices." — Session resumption and hardware crypto acceleration make it feasible on many modern microcontrollers.
  • "OAuth2 replaces the need for device-level authentication." — OAuth2 device flows typically authorize a device into a user account, layered on top of, not instead of, device identity.

16. Glossary

Mutual TLS (mTLS)
A TLS handshake variant where both client and server present certificates, authenticating each other.
Session resumption
Reusing an abbreviated handshake to re-establish a secure session without repeating the full asymmetric handshake.
Pre-shared key (PSK)
A symmetric key established out-of-band and shared between communicating parties before a session begins.

17. Self-Assessment

1. What is the main operational risk of using a single pre-shared key across an entire device fleet?

A shared fleet-wide key means one extracted key equals a fleet-wide compromise, with no way to revoke a single device without changing the key everywhere.
