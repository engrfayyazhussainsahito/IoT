Containment, Eradication & Recovery Runbooks for IoT/OT

Write a containment runbook that avoids unsafe physical-world side effects.

35 min read Level: MS foundation → advanced Status: Full lesson

1. Introduction

This lesson turns Week 12's principles into the actual documents a responder follows during an incident: runbooks specific enough to execute under pressure, but built around the safety-first and physical-plausibility considerations the rest of this week has established.

2. Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Write a containment runbook that avoids unsafe physical-world side effects.
  • Plan recovery steps that restore both function and trust in a device.

3. Prerequisites

Week 0 foundations, plus Weeks 0–11 of this course.

4. Core Concepts

Safe containment actions for actuating devices: rather than a single generic "isolate the device" instruction, an effective runbook specifies containment options ranked by disruption level — logical network isolation (least disruptive, may not fully stop an active compromise), controlled shutdown sequence (more disruptive, safer for the physical process), and full physical disconnection (most disruptive, reserved for confirmed severe compromise) — matched to the specific asset's actual operational context.

Re-provisioning vs. re-flashing: eradication for a compromised IoT device might mean simply re-provisioning it with new credentials (Week 3/10's key-rotation material) if the compromise was credential-based, or a full firmware re-flash (Week 4's OTA material) if the compromise involved actual code execution or persistence — choosing the wrong, lighter-weight option risks leaving the actual root cause unaddressed.

Verifying eradication on constrained hardware is genuinely harder than on a server: without the rich tooling available on a general-purpose OS, confirming "this device is now clean" often relies on attestation (Week 9) and a known-good firmware hash comparison, rather than a comprehensive malware scan the device simply can't run.

7. Mathematical / Formal Foundations

No formula — instead, an eradication-scope decision rule: if the compromise involved only credential misuse with no evidence of code execution or persistence, re-provisioning may suffice; any evidence of code execution, unexpected firmware behavior, or unexplained persistence should default to a full re-flash, since partial remediation risks leaving a foothold in place.

8. Practical Examples

Worked example: an investigation determines a device was compromised purely through a weak, reused password with no evidence of firmware tampering. Re-provisioning with a new, unique credential and confirming via attestation that the firmware hash matches the known-good baseline is a proportionate eradication step — a full re-flash would be unnecessary disruption for a purely credential-based compromise.

10. Security Perspective

A one-size-fits-all "isolate everything, reflash everything" incident response policy is operationally simpler to write but wastes response effort and causes unnecessary disruption compared to a ladder-based, evidence-driven approach — proportionality matters as much in response as it does in the risk-scoring methods from earlier in this course.

11. Industry Perspective

Mature IoT/OT security programs maintain asset-class-specific runbooks rather than one generic incident response document, recognizing that a sensor and a safety-critical actuator warrant very different containment ladders even if both technically fall under "IoT device."

12. Research Perspective

Research into automated, policy-driven containment-action selection (choosing the least-disruptive-sufficient containment step based on real-time evidence) is an active area, aiming to remove some of the time pressure from this decision during a live incident.

13. Case Study

Post-incident reviews have repeatedly found that responders defaulted to maximum-disruption containment (full physical disconnection) even in cases where a lighter step would have sufficed, simply because no asset-specific runbook existed to guide a more proportionate response under time pressure.

14. Common Misconceptions

  • "The most aggressive containment action is always the safest choice." — Over-aggressive containment can itself cause unnecessary operational disruption or, in some cases, an unsafe abrupt shutdown.
  • "Re-provisioning and re-flashing are interchangeable eradication options." — They address different root causes; choosing the lighter option for a code-execution compromise risks leaving a foothold in place.
  • "You can always run a full malware scan to verify eradication." — Many constrained devices lack the tooling for this; attestation and known-good hash comparison are the realistic alternative.

16. Glossary

Containment ladder
A ranked set of containment actions from least to most operationally disruptive, matched to the severity of a confirmed compromise.
Re-provisioning
Reissuing a device's credentials or identity material as a remediation step, without necessarily replacing its firmware.

17. Self-Assessment

1. Why might re-provisioning (issuing new credentials) be insufficient eradication for a compromise involving evidence of code execution?

Re-provisioning addresses credential-based compromise but doesn't remove persistent malicious code; evidence of code execution generally requires a full re-flash to ensure genuine eradication.
