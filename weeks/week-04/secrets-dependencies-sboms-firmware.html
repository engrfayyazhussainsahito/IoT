Secrets, Dependencies & SBOMs for Firmware

Produce a software bill of materials for a firmware image.

35 min read Level: MS foundation → advanced Status: Full lesson

1. Introduction

A firmware image is a small universe of third-party code, vendor SDKs, and — too often — secrets that were never meant to ship. Knowing exactly what's inside your own firmware is a surprisingly recent and still-incomplete industry practice.

2. Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Produce a software bill of materials for a firmware image.
  • Identify hardcoded secrets and vendored-library risk in firmware.

3. Prerequisites

Week 0 foundations, plus Weeks 0–3 of this course.

4. Core Concepts

A Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) is a structured inventory of every component in a software or firmware build — direct dependencies, transitive dependencies, and their versions. Standard formats include SPDX and CycloneDX. Without an SBOM, a team often cannot answer a simple question quickly: "are we affected by the vulnerability just announced in library X?"

Vendored SDKs — chip-vendor-provided libraries bundled directly into a project — are a particularly under-tracked dependency category. They're often copied in wholesale rather than managed through a package manager, making them easy to forget when auditing for known vulnerabilities, and they can lag years behind upstream security fixes.

Secret scanning firmware images (searching compiled binaries for hardcoded API keys, private keys, or credentials) catches a surprisingly common and severe class of finding — secrets left in debug builds, forgotten test credentials, or keys embedded for convenience during development.

7. Mathematical / Formal Foundations

No formula — instead, a coverage question: what percentage of your firmware's actual code (by line count or binary size) is represented in your SBOM? Teams that measure this for the first time frequently find their SBOM covers well under half the actual shipped code, with vendored SDKs as the largest gap.

8. Practical Examples

Worked example: a newly announced CVE affects a specific version of a TLS library. A team with a complete SBOM can answer "are we affected, and on which products/firmware versions?" within minutes. A team without one may need days of manual code archaeology across every product line to answer the same question — a real, measurable incident-response cost of skipping SBOM practice.

# Example: scanning a firmware binary for potential hardcoded secrets

10. Security Perspective

An SBOM's real value shows up during incident response, not during normal development — it's the difference between a same-day "not affected, confirmed" answer and a days-long scramble every time a new vulnerability is disclosed in a widely used library.

11. Industry Perspective

SBOM requirements are increasingly appearing in procurement contracts and regulation (Week 14 covers this in depth) — customers, especially government and critical-infrastructure buyers, are starting to require an SBOM as a condition of purchase.

12. Research Perspective

Automated SBOM generation directly from compiled firmware binaries (rather than relying on accurate build-time manifests) is an active tooling research area, since build-time SBOMs can miss vendored code that bypassed normal dependency management.

13. Case Study

Widely publicized library vulnerabilities (in the style of major open-source supply-chain incidents) have repeatedly caught firmware vendors without a clear answer to "are we using this library, and where" — driving the current push toward SBOM adoption industry-wide.

14. Common Misconceptions

  • "SBOMs are only relevant for cloud/server software, not firmware." — Firmware's long support tail and vendored-SDK sprawl make SBOM tracking arguably more valuable, not less.
  • "If it's not in our version control, it's not part of our attack surface." — Vendored SDKs and pre-built libraries are part of the shipped attack surface regardless of how they're tracked internally.
  • "Secret scanning is only necessary for public repositories." — Compiled firmware binaries that ship to customers can leak secrets just as easily as a public code repository.

16. Glossary

SBOM
Software Bill of Materials — a structured inventory of all components in a software or firmware build.
Vendored SDK
