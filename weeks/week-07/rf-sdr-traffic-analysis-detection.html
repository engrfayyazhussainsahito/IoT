RF & SDR-Based Traffic Analysis and Detection

Capture and inspect IoT RF traffic with a software-defined radio.

35 min read Level: MS foundation → advanced Status: Full lesson

1. Introduction

Everything in the previous lesson about protocol weaknesses is only useful if you can actually observe the traffic in question. A software-defined radio turns a general-purpose computer into a flexible receiver (and, carefully and legally, a transmitter) capable of capturing and analyzing almost any IoT RF signal.

2. Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Capture and inspect IoT RF traffic with a software-defined radio.
  • Identify anomalous RF patterns that suggest jamming or replay.

3. Prerequisites

Week 0 foundations, plus Weeks 0–6 of this course.

4. Core Concepts

An SDR (Software-Defined Radio) digitizes raw radio signals and hands them to software for processing, rather than relying on protocol-specific hardware — the same $20-30 device can capture BLE, Zigbee, LoRa, or arbitrary ISM-band traffic depending on the software configuration, making it the single most versatile tool in an IoT security lab.

Spectrum analysis — viewing signal strength across a frequency range over time (a waterfall display) — is often the fastest way to spot anomalies: unexpected transmissions, unusually frequent retransmissions (which might indicate jamming-induced packet loss), or signals appearing on frequencies where nothing should be transmitting.

Replay and jamming signatures have recognizable patterns: replay attacks often show identical waveforms repeated at unusual intervals; jamming shows sustained energy across a target frequency band, distinguishable from legitimate burst-style IoT transmissions.

7. Mathematical / Formal Foundations

No formula — instead, a Nyquist-driven practical constraint: an SDR's sample rate must be at least twice the bandwidth of the signal you're trying to capture faithfully. A cheap SDR's typical 2-3 MHz usable bandwidth easily covers a single BLE or Zigbee channel, but capturing wider Wi-Fi-adjacent signals requires more capable (and expensive) hardware.

8. Practical Examples

Worked example: a waterfall display shows repeated, identical bursts on a garage-door opener's frequency at suspiciously regular intervals with no corresponding legitimate use — a strong candidate for either an automated replay attack in progress or a misbehaving nearby device, both worth investigating further with a protocol-level decode.

9. Code / Worked Example

RF ANOMALY TRIAGE CHECKLIST
[ ] Capture a baseline of NORMAL traffic on the target frequency first
[ ] Compare suspicious activity against that baseline waterfall
[ ] Sustained energy across the band -> possible jamming
[ ] Identical repeated waveforms at irregular but repeated intervals
    -> possible replay attack
[ ] Unexpected transmissions on frequencies with no known legitimate
    device -> investigate unknown/rogue transmitter
[ ] Decode captured packets at the protocol level to confirm hypothesis
    before concluding malicious activity (rule out benign causes first)

10. Security Perspective

RF monitoring is one of the few detection capabilities that can catch an attack at the physical layer, before it ever reaches a network log — valuable specifically because many IoT attacks (jamming, replay) leave no trace at all in conventional network-based monitoring.

11. Industry Perspective

Physical security and facilities teams increasingly deploy dedicated RF monitoring appliances for exactly this reason — detecting rogue transmitters, jamming attempts, or unauthorized wireless devices on a site that traditional IT security tooling has no visibility into.

12. Research Perspective

Academic research into RF fingerprinting (identifying individual transmitters by subtle hardware-specific signal characteristics, beyond just protocol-level identifiers) is an active area with applications in both attack detection and device authentication.

13. Case Study

Documented vehicle and garage-door relay/replay research has relied heavily on SDR-based capture and analysis exactly like the workflow in this lesson, demonstrating real-world practicality of the technique for both defenders monitoring their own systems and researchers studying vulnerable products.

14. Common Misconceptions

  • "You need expensive, specialized hardware to do meaningful RF analysis." — A basic $20-30 SDR dongle can capture most common IoT protocols effectively for initial analysis.
  • "Any unusual RF activity means an attack is happening." — Legitimate causes (misconfigured devices, nearby unrelated equipment) are common; always rule these out before concluding malicious activity.
  • "RF monitoring can replace network-based detection." — They're complementary; RF monitoring catches physical-layer issues that never reach the network, but network monitoring catches issues invisible at the RF layer.
