Reconnaissance, Enumeration & Attack Surface Mapping (devices + RF)

Enumerate a device's exposed interfaces, ports, and RF footprint.

35 min read Level: MS foundation → advanced Status: Full lesson

1. Introduction

Once scope is agreed, reconnaissance is where a pentest actually begins — and for IoT/OT targets, that means enumerating across three simultaneous dimensions this course has built up separately: hardware interfaces (Week 6), network services (Week 7), and RF footprint (also Week 7).

2. Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Enumerate a device's exposed interfaces, ports, and RF footprint.
  • Build an attack-surface map spanning hardware, firmware, and network.

3. Prerequisites

Week 0 foundations, plus Weeks 0–12 of this course.

4. Core Concepts

Interface and port enumeration combines Week 6's hardware reconnaissance (finding debug pads, identifying buses) with more familiar network service enumeration (open ports, exposed APIs) — a comprehensive IoT attack surface map needs both, since either alone gives an incomplete picture.

RF footprint mapping uses Week 7's SDR techniques specifically for reconnaissance purposes: identifying what frequencies and protocols a target device actually uses in practice (which may differ from its datasheet's full capability list, since many chips support more protocols than a given product actually enables).

Consolidated attack-surface diagrams bring hardware, network, and RF findings into a single artifact — echoing Week 1's trust-boundary mapping and Week 8's midterm methodology, now populated with actual reconnaissance data rather than architectural assumptions.

7. Mathematical / Formal Foundations

No formula — instead, a coverage-completeness question for any reconnaissance report: does it address hardware, network, AND RF, or does it default to whichever dimension the tester happens to be most comfortable with? A report strong on network enumeration but silent on hardware/RF findings likely reflects tester skill gaps, not target reality.

8. Practical Examples

Worked example: network enumeration alone finds a device exposing only a well-secured HTTPS API, suggesting a small attack surface. RF reconnaissance, however, reveals the device also communicates over an unencrypted proprietary sub-GHz protocol for a "convenience" pairing feature not mentioned in public documentation — a much larger attack surface only visible once all three reconnaissance dimensions were actually performed.

9. Code / Worked Example

CONSOLIDATED IOT RECONNAISSANCE CHECKLIST
Hardware:
[ ] All exposed pads/headers identified and bus-typed (Week 6 method)
[ ] Chip part numbers identified where possible (datasheet capability
    check against actually-enabled features)
Network:
[ ] All open ports/services enumerated
[ ] All exposed APIs (local and cloud-facing) documented
RF:
[ ] Actual transmitted frequencies/protocols captured via SDR,
    not just assumed from datasheet/marketing material
[ ] All three dimensions consolidated into a single attack-surface
    diagram before proceeding to vulnerability validation

10. Security Perspective

Reconnaissance gaps are attack-surface blind spots by definition — a vulnerability assessment can only be as complete as its reconnaissance, and IoT's multi-dimensional attack surface makes single-dimension reconnaissance (network-only, most commonly) a systematic source of missed findings.

11. Industry Perspective

Specialized IoT security testing firms typically staff engagements with both hardware and network/RF specialists precisely because comprehensive reconnaissance across all three dimensions rarely lives comfortably within a single tester's primary skill set.

12. Research Perspective

Academic IoT security assessments increasingly document their reconnaissance methodology explicitly across hardware, network, and RF dimensions, reflecting a maturing standard for what constitutes a genuinely comprehensive IoT security assessment.

13. Case Study

Documented IoT device assessments have repeatedly found their most significant vulnerabilities in a reconnaissance dimension (often RF or hardware) that a network-focused-only assessment would have entirely missed — directly validating the consolidated, three-dimensional approach this lesson recommends.

14. Common Misconceptions

  • "Network enumeration alone gives a reasonably complete IoT attack surface picture." — Hardware and RF dimensions frequently reveal significant additional attack surface invisible to network-only reconnaissance.
  • "A device's actual RF behavior always matches its datasheet's full capability list." — Products often enable only a subset of a chip's capabilities, or add undocumented proprietary protocols not evident from datasheets alone.
  • "Reconnaissance is a quick, preliminary step that doesn't need much rigor." — Incomplete reconnaissance directly limits the completeness of every subsequent finding.

16. Glossary

Attack surface mapping
The process of identifying and documenting all potential points of entry or interaction with a target system.
RF footprint
The set of frequencies, protocols, and communication patterns a device actually uses in practice.

17. Self-Assessment

1. Why might an IoT device assessment relying only on network enumeration miss significant attack surface?

IoT devices often have attack surface spanning hardware debug interfaces and RF protocols that network-based enumeration alone cannot discover, requiring a multi-dimensional reconnaissance approach.
