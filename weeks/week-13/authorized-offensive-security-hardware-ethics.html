Authorized Offensive Security From First Principles (hardware RoE/ethics)

Explain why hardware pentesting rules of engagement differ from web/app pentests.

35 min read Level: MS foundation → advanced Status: Full lesson

1. Introduction

Week 0 set the ground rules for lab work on your own hardware. Week 13 raises the stakes: authorized offensive testing against someone else's product or deployment, where the rules of engagement carry legal weight and the physical nature of the target introduces risks a web pentest never faces.

2. Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Explain why hardware pentesting rules of engagement differ from web/app pentests.
  • Recognize destructive-testing risks unique to physical devices.

3. Prerequisites

Week 0 foundations, plus Weeks 0–12 of this course.

4. Core Concepts

Hardware-specific RoE considerations that don't arise in web/app pentesting: a physical device can be irreversibly damaged by a test (unlike a web server, which can usually be restored from a snapshot), test units are often limited in supply (sometimes just one borrowed or purchased unit), and some techniques (fault injection, chip decapping) are inherently destructive by nature, not just risky.

Destructive vs. non-destructive testing needs explicit classification and client sign-off before testing begins — a client may authorize non-destructive firmware analysis but explicitly withhold authorization for chip-level extraction that would destroy the only test unit available.

Client communication for physical risk means setting expectations differently than a web engagement: "there is a nonzero chance this specific test could permanently damage the device" is a sentence a hardware pentest client needs to hear and explicitly accept before that test proceeds, in writing.

7. Mathematical / Formal Foundations

No formula — instead, a risk-disclosure completeness check: for every technique in your planned test methodology, can you state in one sentence what happens to the physical device if that specific technique fails or damages the unit? If you can't answer that for a given technique, you haven't fully assessed its physical risk yet.

8. Practical Examples

Worked example: a client provides a single unit of a new IoT product for a pre-launch security assessment. The tester identifies that JTAG access requires desoldering a shield can, which is irreversible. Rather than proceeding under general test authorization, the tester specifically flags this step to the client, explains the alternative (non-destructive testing only, with reduced coverage), and gets written sign-off before deciding how to proceed.

10. Security Perspective

Hardware pentest engagements that skip explicit destructive-action sign-off risk a damaged client relationship even when the testing itself is technically sound — the physical, sometimes irreversible nature of hardware testing raises the communication bar compared to typical software pentesting.

11. Industry Perspective

Established hardware security consultancies build destructive-vs-non-destructive classification directly into their standard scoping templates and contracts, treating it as a routine part of engagement setup rather than an ad hoc conversation per engagement.

12. Research Perspective

Hardware security research ethics discussions (echoing Week 1's disclosure-ethics lesson) extend naturally to commercial pentesting: the same "what happens if this doesn't work as planned" discipline applies whether the context is academic research or a paid client engagement.

13. Case Study

Documented hardware security consulting post-mortems have cited unclear destructive-testing authorization as a source of client disputes, reinforcing why leading firms treat this as a mandatory, explicit, separately-documented sign-off rather than an implicit part of general scope.

14. Common Misconceptions

  • "General test authorization implicitly covers any technique needed to complete the assessment." — Destructive techniques warrant their own explicit, separate sign-off given their irreversible nature.
  • "Hardware pentesting risk communication is basically the same as web pentesting." — The possibility of permanent physical damage to a limited-supply test unit is a distinct risk category requiring its own discussion.
  • "If a technique is standard practice in hardware security research, it doesn't need special client authorization." — Standard practice among researchers doesn't substitute for a specific client's informed consent for their specific unit.

16. Glossary

Rules of Engagement (RoE)
The documented scope, boundaries, and authorized techniques for a security testing engagement.
Destructive testing
A testing technique that irreversibly damages or alters the physical device under test.

17. Self-Assessment

1. Why do hardware pentest engagements often require separate, explicit sign-off for destructive testing techniques?

Unlike software testing, hardware testing can permanently and irreversibly damage a physical unit, often one of limited supply — a risk category serious enough to warrant explicit, separate client consent.
