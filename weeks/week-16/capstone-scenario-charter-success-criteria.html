Capstone Scenario Charter & Success Criteria

Interpret a capstone scenario charter and its grading criteria.

40 min read Level: MS foundation → advanced Status: Full lesson

1. Introduction

The capstone is the course's final integration point: a multi-week project spanning assessment, defense, incident response, governance, and career reflection. This first lesson is about reading the charter correctly — because a misunderstood scope is the single most common reason capstone projects run into trouble.

2. Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Interpret a capstone scenario charter and its grading criteria.
  • Plan a multi-week capstone deliverable schedule.

3. Prerequisites

Week 0 foundations, plus Weeks 0–15 of this course.

4. Core Concepts

A well-formed capstone charter has four parts, and you should be able to locate each one before writing anything: the fictional organization's profile (size, industry, device fleet), the specific scenario driving the work (an assessment request, an incident, a compliance deadline), explicit success criteria tied to the grading rubric, and constraints (timeline, assumed team size, what's out of scope).

7. Mathematical / Formal Foundations

No formula — instead, a scope-clarity check: for every sentence in the charter, ask "does this define scope, or just flavor?" Confusing narrative color for a scope requirement is the fastest way to over- or under-deliver.

8. Practical Examples

Worked example: a charter states "Northwind Facilities operates 40 smart buildings across 3 regions." That's flavor, establishing scale. A line reading "your assessment must cover the door-control and HVAC subsystems only" is scope — and going beyond it (say, also assessing the fire-suppression system) may cost you time without earning credit.

9. Code / Worked Example

CHARTER READING CHECKLIST
[ ] Organization profile identified (industry, scale, device types)
[ ] Triggering scenario identified (why is this work happening now?)
[ ] Success criteria mapped 1:1 to the grading rubric
[ ] Explicit out-of-scope items noted
[ ] Timeline and milestone dates extracted into your own planning doc

10. Security Perspective

Scope discipline here mirrors professional consulting engagements exactly: work outside an agreed scope isn't "extra value," it's scope creep that can introduce risk (touching systems you weren't authorized to touch) or simply waste billable time.

11. Industry Perspective

Real security engagements almost always start with a charter or statement-of-work document with the same four components — this lesson is training you to read that document type, not just this course's version of it.

13. Case Study

A common capstone pitfall from past cohorts: spending disproportionate effort on a technically interesting but out-of-scope subsystem mentioned only as background color in the charter, at the cost of the in-scope deliverables that are actually graded.

14. Common Misconceptions

  • "More scope covered always means a better grade." — Grading follows the rubric and stated success criteria, not raw effort or scope covered.
  • "The charter's narrative details are just flavor text to skim." — Some narrative details encode real scope or constraint information.

16. Glossary

Charter
A document establishing a project's scenario, scope, constraints, and success criteria.
Scope creep
Unplanned expansion of a project's work beyond its originally agreed boundaries.
