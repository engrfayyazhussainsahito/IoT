Physical Security, Tamper Resistance & Anti-Tamper Design

Evaluate a device enclosure for tamper-evidence and tamper-resistance.

35 min read Level: MS foundation → advanced Status: Full lesson

1. Introduction

Every mitigation this week assumes an attacker has to work to get physical access to sensitive internals. Enclosure design is what actually creates — or fails to create — that assumption's precondition, and it's where security engineering meets industrial design most directly.

2. Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Evaluate a device enclosure for tamper-evidence and tamper-resistance.
  • Explain the difference between tamper-evident and tamper-resistant design.

3. Prerequisites

Week 0 foundations, plus Weeks 0–5 of this course.

4. Core Concepts

Tamper-evident design doesn't prevent tampering — it ensures tampering leaves visible evidence (a seal that visibly breaks, a case that can't be reassembled without a mark). This shifts the defense from "prevent" to "detect after the fact," useful when the goal is accountability or triggering an investigation rather than absolute prevention.

Tamper-resistant design actively raises the cost or difficulty of accessing internals: potting compound (epoxy encasing a circuit board, making component-level access destructive), a fine wire mesh embedded in an enclosure that triggers a zeroization circuit if cut, or simply a case requiring specialized tools and substantial time to open non-destructively.

Zeroization — actively erasing sensitive key material the instant tampering is detected — is the strongest response tier, converting a physical compromise attempt into a fail-safe rather than a fail-open condition, at the cost of the device becoming permanently unusable once triggered.

7. Mathematical / Formal Foundations

No formula — instead, a cost/threat-model matching exercise: tamper protection cost should scale with the value of what's protected and the sophistication of the expected attacker, not be applied uniformly. Potting a $500 industrial controller's key-storage chip is a reasonable investment; potting a $10 consumer sensor's entire board rarely is.

8. Practical Examples

Worked example: a payment terminal uses a fine conductive mesh embedded in its case, wired to a always-on, battery-backed monitoring circuit. Cutting through the case to reach internal components breaks the mesh, immediately triggering zeroization of stored cryptographic keys — rendering the device cryptographically useless to an attacker even if they successfully gain physical access moments later.

10. Security Perspective

Tamper protection should be threat-model-driven, not a default "more is always better" checkbox — over-investing in physical protection for a low-value asset wastes manufacturing budget that could fund other, more impactful controls elsewhere in the product.

11. Industry Perspective

Payment industry standards (PCI PTS) mandate specific, audited tamper-response requirements including active zeroization for devices handling payment card data — one of the clearest examples of regulation directly driving a specific tamper-protection tier.

12. Research Perspective

Research into cost-effective tamper-evidence for lower-cost consumer devices (where full active zeroization is economically infeasible) continues to explore cheaper detection mechanisms that still provide meaningful accountability value.

13. Case Study

Payment-terminal tamper-response mechanisms have been specifically studied and, in some documented cases, defeated by well-resourced attackers exploiting gaps in mesh coverage or response-circuit battery-backup failures — illustrating that even the highest tamper-protection tier requires careful, complete implementation to deliver its intended guarantee.

14. Common Misconceptions

  • "Tamper-evident and tamper-resistant mean the same thing." — Tamper-evident detects and reveals tampering after the fact; tamper-resistant actively raises the difficulty of tampering in the first place.
  • "Every IoT device needs active zeroization." — This tier's cost is usually only justified for high-value assets like payment or cryptographic key material, not general consumer sensors.
  • "Potting a board makes it completely un-analyzable." — Potting raises cost and difficulty significantly but sophisticated attackers have documented techniques (chemical, mechanical) for removing potting compound.

16. Glossary

Tamper-evident
Design that ensures tampering leaves visible evidence, without necessarily preventing the tampering itself.
Tamper-resistant
Design that actively raises the cost or difficulty of accessing or modifying a device's internals.
Zeroization
The active, immediate erasure of sensitive key material upon detection of tampering or compromise.

17. Self-Assessment

1. What is the key difference between tamper-evident and tamper-resistant design?

Tamper-evident design shifts the goal to detection/accountability (visible evidence of tampering), while tamper-resistant design actively raises the cost and difficulty of successful tampering.
